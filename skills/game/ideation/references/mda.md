# Player fantasy, from feeling back to mechanics

Source: Hunicke, LeBlanc and Zubek, "MDA: A Formal Approach to Game Design and Game Research" (2004). The paper defines Mechanics as the game's components at the level of data representation and algorithms, Dynamics as the run-time behavior of the mechanics acting on player input and each other over time, and Aesthetics as the desirable emotional responses evoked in the player. Designers see mechanics give rise to dynamics and then aesthetics; players meet aesthetics first, then dynamics, then mechanics. The paper itself starts with aesthetics, so design from the end.

1. Aesthetics: pick one or two target feelings from the paper's taxonomy, which it calls a starting vocabulary and not a complete list: sensation (game as sense-pleasure), fantasy (make-believe), narrative (drama), challenge (obstacle course), fellowship (social framework), discovery (uncharted territory), expression (self-discovery), submission (pastime); or use the user's own words. Games usually pursue several at once, in different degrees. Ask: "after ten minutes, what should the player say they felt?"
2. Player fantasy: one sentence, "You are ___ doing ___", that carries the feeling.
3. Dynamics: which behaviors would produce that feeling (hoarding, risk-taking, improvising, rushing and recovering)? List two or three.
4. Mechanics: the smallest set of rules and verbs that would make those dynamics appear. Stop at three mechanics (this limit is this skill's, not the paper's).
5. Check backward: would these mechanics plausibly produce the feeling, or something else? Name the risk.

Output for the pitch: the target feeling, the fantasy sentence, the dynamics wanted, the mechanics chosen.
